## Due: December 2, 2024

## Overview

A healthy project backlog is essential for continuity and community engagement. As your semester winds down, this checkpoint asks you to audit and clean up your project's backlog to ensure it's ready for future internal teams and external open source contributors. A well-maintained backlog helps new contributors understand what needs to be done, makes onboarding easier, and keeps your project's momentum going beyond your time as Tech Lead.

## Learning Objectives

By completing this checkpoint, you will:
- Evaluate the current state of your project backlog
- Practice backlog hygiene and issue management
- Prepare work items for different contributor types (internal teams vs. external contributors)
- Document project priorities and future direction
- Set up your project for successful handoff and continued development

## Background: Why Backlog Health Matters

A backlog serves multiple audiences:
- **Future internal teams**: Need clear, actionable work items to pick up where you left off
- **External contributors**: Need well-defined issues they can tackle independently
- **Project stakeholders**: Need visibility into priorities and progress
- **Your future self**: Documentation of what you learned and what still needs attention
Dead or unclear issues create friction. A cluttered backlog makes it hard to find meaningful work. Missing issues mean opportunities get lost. This audit ensures your backlog is a useful tool rather than a source of confusion.

## Assignment

Conduct a comprehensive audit of your project backlog and clean it up to support future development. Your audit should:

### 1. Remove "Dead" Issues

- Close issues that are no longer relevant
- Close duplicates
- Archive or close issues blocked by external factors unlikely to change
- Document why issues were closed (brief comment explaining the decision)

### 2. Update Existing Issues

- Ensure issue titles clearly describe the work
- Add or update descriptions with sufficient context
- Add appropriate labels (e.g., bug, enhancement, good first issue, documentation)
- Update status if work has been partially completed
- Link related issues or PRs
- Clarify acceptance criteria where ambiguous

### 3. Add Missing Issues

- Document known bugs or technical debt
- Create issues for planned features or improvements
- Break down large work items into smaller, actionable issues
- Add issues that would be good for external contributors (clearly scoped, well-defined)

### 4. Organize and Prioritize

- Use your GitHub Project board to reflect current priorities
- Ensure high-priority items are clearly marked
- Group related issues (using labels, milestones, or project columns)
- Identify what should be tackled next by internal teams vs. what's suitable for external contributors

### 5. Prepare for Handoff

- Ensure there's enough defined work for the next internal dev team to start productively
- Tag issues appropriate for external contributors with labels like "good first issue" or "help wanted"
- Document any context future teams need (technical decisions, gotchas, dependencies)

## Guidelines

**Focus on utility over perfection**: The goal is a backlog that's useful, not exhaustive documentation of every possible feature.
**Be realistic about scope**: Don't create issues for work that's unlikely to happen. Focus on actionable items.
**Think about contributor experience**: Would a new team member understand what needs to be done from reading the issue?
**Document your decisions**: Brief comments explaining why you closed or deprioritized issues help future teams understand your reasoning.

## Deliverable

Your **primary documentation is your GitHub Issues and GitHub Project**. Ensure they accurately reflect the current state and priorities of your project.
Additionally, submit a brief summary document (1-3 pages) suitable for inclusion in your final product strategy that contains:
- **Audit Summary**:
- How many issues were closed, updated, or added
- Major changes to backlog organization
- Any significant discoveries during the audit
- **Priority Overview**:
- Top 3-5 priorities for the next internal dev team
- 3-5 issues well-suited for external contributors
- **Handoff Notes**:
- Critical context future teams need to know
- Known technical debt or challenges
- Recommendations for next steps
- **Links**:
- Link to your GitHub Issues
- Link to your GitHub Project board

### Format

- PDF document uploaded to checkpoint submission
- Should be scannable and concise (bullet points are fine)
- Focus on information not already obvious from your GitHub Issues/Project

## What Makes a Strong Submission

- **Backlog reflects current reality**: Issues are accurate and up-to-date
- **Clear prioritization**: Future teams can quickly identify what matters most
- **Contributor-friendly**: Issues have enough context for someone unfamiliar with the project
- **Realistic scope**: Backlog contains work that's actually achievable and relevant
- **Thoughtful curation**: Shows you've made deliberate decisions about what to keep, remove, or add
- **Useful handoff notes**: Captures institutional knowledge that would otherwise be lost

## Notes

- **This is not busywork**: A clean backlog directly benefits your project's future and makes life easier for whoever picks up your work.
- **Quality over quantity**: A small number of well-defined issues is better than a large number of vague ones.
- **It's okay to have uncertainty**: If you're unsure about something, document the question in the issue or handoff notes.
- **Think long-term**: You're setting up your project for sustainability beyond this semester.
