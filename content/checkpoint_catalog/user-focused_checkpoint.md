**Due:** October 23
**Sprint:** 4
**Focus:** User Validation

### Overview

Building software without involving users is a recipe for solving the wrong problems or building unusable solutions. This checkpoint requires you to conduct user research that validates whether your work actually helps real users accomplish their goals.

### Requirements

## Conduct user research, then synthesize your findings:

- **Identify Your Users** Work with your client to identify and recruit participants:
- Who are the actual or intended users of your tool?
- Your client can help identify users, but **the client alone is not sufficient**—you must involve people who will actually use what you're building
- For early-stage products: recruit prospective users who currently do the work your tool aims to support
- For mature products: recruit current or potential users
- **Conduct User Research** Choose an approach appropriate to your product's maturity:
**Observation/Usability Testing** (preferred):
- Watch users attempt realistic tasks with your tool
- Observe where they struggle, what confuses them, what works well
- Ask users to think aloud as they work
- Take detailed notes on their behavior, not just their opinions
**User Interviews**:
- Understand the problem space and user needs
- Validate assumptions about pain points and workflows
- Explore how users currently solve the problem
- For early products: validate the problem exists and matters
**Prototype Testing**:
- For early-stage products: test paper prototypes, wireframes, or minimal implementations
- Walk through user journeys and gather feedback
- Focus on "Can users accomplish the core task?"
**Scale**: Anything is better than nothing, but aim for quality over quantity:
- Minimum: 1 substantive session
- Ideal: 3-5 deep sessions with different users
- **Document Your Research** Capture what happened:
- **Research notes**: What you observed, what users said, what surprised you
- **Design artifacts**: Screenshots, task flows, notes about specific UI elements tested
- **Raw evidence**: Quotes, behavioral observations, usability issues discovered
- **Recordings** (if possible): Audio/video recordings of sessions (with participant consent)
- **Synthesize Findings** Analyze and communicate what you learned (1-3 pages):
**Research Overview**:
- Who did you talk to? (roles, backgrounds, relationship to the problem)
- What method(s) did you use?
- What tasks or scenarios did you test?
**Key Findings**:
- **Problem validation**: Does this solve a real problem users have?
- **Usability validation**: Can people actually use this? Where do they struggle?
- **Value validation**: Does this provide meaningful value to users?
**Evidence**:
- Specific examples, quotes, or observations that illustrate your findings
- What worked well? What didn't?
- What surprised you?
**Implications**:
- What changes should you make based on this research?
- What assumptions were validated or invalidated?
- What do you need to learn next?
- If findings are immediately actionable, note what you'll address in upcoming sprints
**Format Resources**: For examples of user research report formats, see:
- Nielsen Norman Group: Responsible UX Research Reports
- User Interviews: Research Report Template
- Maze: How to Write a UX Research Report

### Client Involvement

Your client should be involved in this process:
- **Minimum**: Help identify users and review your findings
- **Ideal**: Participate in research sessions to observe firsthand

### Deliverables

- **Research notes and artifacts**: Your observation notes, interview guides, screenshots, recordings (if available), or other documentation of what you did
- **Synthesis document** (1-3 pages): A clear summary of findings and implications, following the structure above

### Evaluation Criteria

- **User Engagement** (1 point): Evidence of genuine engagement with real users beyond just the client
- **Research Quality** (1 point): Appropriate methods, good questions, detailed observations, focus on behavior and usability
- **Synthesis & Insight** (1 point): Clear findings supported by evidence, actionable implications for the project

### Notes

- If your product isn't ready for users yet, test concepts, prototypes, or workflows—but involve real users
- Focus on observing what people *do*, not just what they *say*
- It's okay to discover your approach needs significant changes—that's valuable learning
- Document what didn't work as thoroughly as what did
