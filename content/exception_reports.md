When you deviate from an expected norm—missing a deadline or adapting a recommended process—you owe a written account. This document explains what that account looks like, why it matters, and how to write one well.

## Why Exception Reports Exist

In professional settings, exceptions to commitments and processes require transparent communication. A missed deadline doesn't disappear because no one mentioned it. A modified process isn't validated just because it felt right at the time. The written record is what makes the difference between professional judgment and avoidance.

This policy formalizes what the course already expects. The Good Practices module says it directly: *"If completing a good practice doesn't make sense or requires adaptation, please document the rationale for the choice you are making and an explanation of your adaptations."* An exception report is the structured format for that documentation.

**"Exception" means departure from the expected norm—not inherently negative.** A well-reasoned process adaptation is a positive exception. The report exists to make your reasoning visible, not to punish you for deviating.

This is a learning objective. The ability to communicate transparently about variations from plan is a professional skill that distinguishes effective leaders from those who hope problems go unnoticed.

## Two Types of Exceptions

### Deadline Misses

You committed to a date and didn't meet it. The commitment was explicit—published in Canvas, agreed to in a sprint plan, or established by course policy. The report explains what happened and what you did about it.

As a Tech Lead, you are accountable for both your own deadlines and your team's. When your team misses a sprint close or a milestone deadline, you file the report. Individual developers on your team who miss their own deadlines are responsible for their own reports—but you should be aware of patterns and address them in 1:1s.

### Process Adaptations

You deliberately changed how you or your team works. The recommended practice exists for good reasons; your adaptation should be grounded in better reasons for your specific context.

This is encouraged when justified. The course explicitly values contextual judgment over blind compliance—that's why we say "Good Practices" instead of "Best Practices." But adaptation without documentation is indistinguishable from ignorance. The report is what makes the difference.

**Examples**: You restructured your retrospective format to better fit your team's dynamics. You changed your code review process to accommodate a team member in a different time zone. You skipped a recommended checkpoint because your project's architecture made it irrelevant—and you can explain why.

## The Exception Report Format

Every exception report includes these elements:

### Summary
One sentence: what varied and when.

> *"Sprint 3 close was submitted two days late on March 5."*
> *"Team retrospective for Sprint 2 used an async written format instead of the recommended synchronous meeting."*

### Context
What was the expectation, and what actually happened? Be specific—reference the deadline, the recommended practice, or the policy.

### Evidence
Concrete artifacts that corroborate your account. These must be **attached or linked**, not just described:

- Commit history and PR timelines
- Slack or Discord message threads
- Work logs or meeting notes
- GitHub project board snapshots
- Calendar entries or scheduling conflicts

"I was busy with other classes" is a claim. Your commit history showing steady work through Tuesday followed by silence through Friday is evidence.

### Reasoning
A causal account connecting circumstances to the outcome. What decisions were made, when, and why? This is the core of the report—walk through the sequence of events and decisions that led to the variation.

### Alternatives Considered
What other options were available, and why were they not selected? This demonstrates that the exception was a deliberate choice—or, in the case of a deadline miss, that you considered recovery options.

### Impact
What was affected—your team, the deliverable, the timeline, the client relationship—and what was done to mitigate the impact.

### Going Forward
For deadline misses: what changes will prevent recurrence? Be concrete—"I'll manage my time better" is not a change.

For process adaptations: what did you learn? Would you make the same choice again? Under what conditions would you revert to the recommended practice?

## Reasoning vs. Rationalization

The difference matters, and it's worth being explicit about it.

**Reasoning** works forward from evidence to conclusion. You observed something, evaluated options, and made a decision. The evidence came first; the conclusion followed.

**Rationalization** works backward from a desired conclusion to cherry-picked justification. You already know what you want to say happened, and you're selecting facts to support it.

A useful test: **could a skeptical peer follow your evidence to the same conclusion?** If your account requires the reader to already agree with you in order to find it persuasive, you're rationalizing.

|                  | Reasoning                        | Rationalization                  |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Direction**    | Evidence → conclusion            | Conclusion → supporting evidence |
| **Completeness** | Includes inconvenient facts      | Omits anything that contradicts  |
| **Tone**         | "Here's what happened"           | "It wasn't my fault because..."  |
| **Test**         | A skeptic would follow the logic | Only works if you already agree  |

**Example — deadline miss:**
- **Reasoning**: "I underestimated the complexity of the CI pipeline configuration. By Wednesday I realized the timeline was at risk and communicated this to the instructor. I prioritized the deployment-blocking issues and deferred the documentation, which I completed by Friday."
- **Rationalization**: "The deadline was unrealistic given everything else going on. Other teams probably had the same issue."

**Example — process adaptation:**
- **Reasoning**: "Our team has two members in significantly different time zones, making synchronous retrospectives consistently exclude someone. We tested an async written format for Sprint 2 and found participation increased from 3/5 to 5/5 team members. We plan to continue this format."
- **Rationalization**: "Nobody likes meetings anyway, and we talk in Slack all the time."

## Timing and Routing

### When to Submit

**Proactive reports**—submitted before or at the time of the variation—are expected and noted favorably. If you know a deadline is at risk, say so before the deadline passes. If you're adapting a process, document the decision when you make it.

**Reactive reports**—submitted after the fact—are accepted but viewed less favorably. Late communication about late work compounds the issue. The exception report itself shouldn't require an exception report.

### Where to Submit

- **Individual exceptions** (your own deadlines, your own process choices): submit directly to the instructor
- **Team-level exceptions** (missed sprint close, adapted team process): you file on behalf of the team as Tech Lead

## How Reports Are Evaluated

Exception reports are acknowledged and factor into performance evaluations. They are not graded as standalone assignments—they exist alongside the work they describe.

**Quality of reasoning matters.** A well-documented variation demonstrates professional maturity. A vague or defensive report suggests the underlying issue hasn't been understood.

**One exception report is normal.** Things happen—schedules conflict, contexts change, reasonable people adapt. A pattern of reports, however, is itself a signal worth examining. If you're filing exception reports every sprint, the exceptions may be symptoms of a deeper issue that needs direct attention.

**There is no approve/deny.** The report is not a permission request. The variation already happened (or is happening). The report makes the reasoning visible so it can be evaluated in context—alongside your other work, your team's trajectory, and the overall pattern of your leadership.
